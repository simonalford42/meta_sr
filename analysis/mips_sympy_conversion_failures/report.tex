\answerbox{Yes: the visible failures occurred \emph{after} the Julia symbolic-regression search had finished.  They occurred while PySR was converting or numerically evaluating its returned expression trees through SymPy.  The MIPS ``ground-truth match'' is not an algebraic comparison to a canonical formula; it is exact behavioral agreement on the complete finite, deduplicated integer transition relation.}

\section{The short version}

The run has two distinct computational worlds:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}c@{\hspace{4pt}}c@{\hspace{4pt}}c@{\hspace{4pt}}c@{\hspace{4pt}}c@{\hspace{4pt}}c@{\hspace{4pt}}c@{}}
\fcolorbox{navy}{bluefill}{\parbox{0.16\textwidth}{\centering Julia search\\expression trees\\training loss}}
& $\longrightarrow$ &
\fcolorbox{navy}{bluefill}{\parbox{0.16\textwidth}{\centering Hall of fame\\Pareto frontier}}
& $\longrightarrow$ &
\fcolorbox{red!70!black}{redfill}{\parbox{0.16\textwidth}{\centering PySR/SymPy\\conversion and\\prediction}}
& $\longrightarrow$ &
\fcolorbox{green!50!black}{greenfill}{\parbox{0.16\textwidth}{\centering Validation then\\full-relation\\exact match}}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The search uses the Julia definitions of operators such as \texttt{mips\_mod},
\texttt{mips\_floordiv}, and \texttt{mips\_max}.  Once the search returns,
Python asks PySR to expose and predict with those expressions.  PySR first
turns the Julia expression into a SymPy expression and a numerical lambda.
That red stage is where the observed failures arise.

The evidence is direct.  For failed task index 0, the worker log says:

\begin{lstlisting}
Starting PySR search: 18 train samples, 3 features
PySR search complete ... in 58.2s
PySR Worker finished ... R^2=-1.0000, ERROR: Failed to evaluate the expression
\end{lstlisting}

The same log prints a Pareto frontier containing a complexity-30 expression
with zero training loss.  Thus the search produced a candidate; the error was
raised afterward.  A different seed for the same scalar relation completed
search in 60.9 seconds and passed the exact checker.

\section{What the 27 scalar problems actually are}

For an RNN with hidden state $h_t$, input $u_t$, and output $o_t$, MIPS first
learns an integer encoding $z_t=E(h_t)\in\mathbb{Z}^d$.  Program extraction is
then decomposed into ordinary scalar relations:

\begin{align}
\text{hidden component }j:&&
  x &= (z_{t-1,1},\ldots,z_{t-1,d},u_t), & y&=z_{t,j},\\
\text{output component }k:&&
  x &= (z_{t,1},\ldots,z_{t,d}), & y&=o_{t,k}.
\end{align}

The raw MIPS traces may contain hundreds of thousands of rows.  We deduplicate
identical inputs and require a unique target for every input.  Only the 27
scalar relations from the ten fully deterministic RNN tasks were sent to
PySR.  Each relation has a fixed 80/20 train/validation split, but the complete
deduplicated table is retained for the final exact check.

This distinction matters: ``complete'' means every distinct encoded relation
row observed in the extracted MIPS data, not every point in an infinite real
domain.

\section{What ``matching'' means}

Let $H$ be PySR's returned Pareto frontier and let $D_{\mathrm{val}}$ and
$D_{\mathrm{full}}$ be the validation and complete deduplicated relations.  For
an expression $e$, define

\[
  A_D(e)=\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{(x_i,y_i)\in D}
  \mathbf{1}\!\left[\operatorname{finite}(e(x_i))\ \wedge\
  |e(x_i)-y_i|\le 10^{-9}\right].
\]

A scalar component is counted as solved exactly when

\[
  \exists e\in H:\quad A_{D_{\mathrm{val}}}(e)=1
  \quad\wedge\quad A_{D_{\mathrm{full}}}(e)=1.
\]

The representation must also have been diagnosed as deterministic.  The
checker considers all useful Pareto-front expressions, not only the one PySR's
``best'' model-selection rule chooses.  There is no symbolic simplification or
formula-identity requirement in the MIPS domain.

\subsection*{Concrete example: alternating-last4 output coordinate 0}

The original trace contains 900,000 rows, but only 11 distinct encoded states.
The full exact relation is:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc@{\qquad}ccc}
\toprule
$x_0$ & $x_1$ & $y$ & $x_0$ & $x_1$ & $y$\\
\midrule
0  & 0 & 0 & 8  & 0 & 0\\
0  & 1 & 1 & 31 & 0 & 0\\
1  & 0 & 0 & 32 & 0 & 0\\
6  & 0 & 0 & 37 & 0 & 0\\
7$^{\dagger}$ & 0 & 0 & 37$^{\dagger}$ & 1 & 1\\
 & & & 38 & 0 & 0\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The daggered rows are the held-out validation rows.  PySR found
$e(x_0,x_1)=x_1$ in all ten completed seeds for this component.  The check is:

\[
  e(7,0)=0,\qquad e(37,1)=1
  \quad\text{(validation)},
\]
followed by the same equality check on all 11 rows.  Because every prediction
equals the integer target, this component receives \texttt{gt\_match\_score=1}.

An original RNN task contains multiple scalar components.  A whole-RNN solve
requires every hidden and output component to be exact; the current evaluator
records the components independently, so whole-task results are grouped later.

\section{Precisely where a post-search failure can occur}

After \texttt{model.fit} returns, the worker performs these operations in order:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \texttt{model.get\_best()} selects a row and may force PySR to create
        SymPy/lambda representations of frontier expressions.
  \item The MIPS checker calls \texttt{model.predict} for each Pareto expression
        on validation rows; only validation-perfect expressions are evaluated
        on the full relation.
  \item Independently of the exact checker, the worker calls
        \texttt{model.predict(X\_val)} for PySR's selected equation to report
        $R^2$ and accuracy.
\end{enumerate}

There are two exception policies:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Inside the MIPS exact checker, a prediction exception for one frontier
        equation is caught and the checker moves to the next equation.
  \item An exception from \texttt{get\_best()} or from the final unconditional
        selected-equation prediction escapes to the outer worker handler and is
        recorded as $R^2=-1$ with an error.
\end{itemize}

Therefore the visible worker errors are definitely post-search, but the current
logs cannot always distinguish \texttt{get\_best()} from the final prediction.
The generic error is truncated, and the temporary hall-of-fame directory is
deleted.  Moreover, a conversion failure \emph{inside} the exact checker can be
silently converted into a non-match.  This is why merely rerunning the rows
explicitly labeled ``error'' is not quite enough for a definitive baseline.

\section{Why the current SymPy mappings are fragile}

The Julia search operators are protected numerically.  For example:

\begin{lstlisting}
mips_mod(x,y) = abs(y) < 1.0e-12 ? 0.0 : mod(x, abs(y))
\end{lstlisting}

The ternary is lazy: when $y$ is zero, Julia never evaluates the modulo branch.
The current SymPy adapter instead constructs

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
Piecewise((0, Eq(y, 0)), (Mod(x, Abs(y)), True))
\end{lstlisting}

Python evaluates function arguments before calling \texttt{Piecewise}.  Thus
the supposedly protected second branch can be constructed and simplified even
when the first branch is true.  Directly exercising the adapter reproduces the
same error classes seen in the partial run:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.92\linewidth}{lYl}
\toprule
Adapter call & Intended protected behavior & Current SymPy result\\
\midrule
\texttt{mips\_mod(x,0)} & return 0 & \texttt{ZeroDivisionError: Modulo by zero}\\
\texttt{mips\_min(nan,x)} & numerical minimum policy & \texttt{ValueError: nan is not comparable}\\
\texttt{mips\_max(zoo,x)} & numerical maximum policy & \texttt{ValueError: zoo is not comparable}\\
\texttt{mips\_lt(nan,x)} & protected numerical predicate & \texttt{TypeError: Invalid NaN comparison}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Nested expressions make these cases reachable even though the dataset inputs
are integers: evolved constants and intermediate division, modulo, minimum, or
maximum nodes can create zero denominators, \texttt{nan}, or complex infinity
(\texttt{zoo}) while SymPy simplifies a frontier expression.

There are also two semantic discrepancies that should be tested during the fix:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Julia treats $|y|<10^{-12}$ as a zero denominator, whereas the SymPy
        adapter protects only exact symbolic equality $y=0$.
  \item Julia's \texttt{mips\_xor} rounds both operands before reducing modulo
        two; the SymPy adapter currently uses $\operatorname{Mod}(x+y,2)$ without
        rounding.  Intermediate expressions need not be integer-valued.
\end{itemize}

The domain also defines safer NumPy operator functions, but this PySR prediction
path calls \texttt{model.predict}; it uses PySR's SymPy/lambda representation,
not that separate NumPy namespace.

\warningbox{A reported exact solve is strong evidence because numerical prediction succeeded on the full relation.  A reported non-solve is less trustworthy in this run: a promising frontier equation may have been skipped after a conversion exception.  The partial solve rate therefore underestimates search performance, and cross-language operator semantics should be checked before treating exact hits as extracted Julia programs.}

\section{Recommended repair and rerun}

The repair should change evaluation, not search:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Represent each MIPS operator as an inert custom SymPy function with an
        explicit numerical implementation, so SymPy does not eagerly rewrite
        protected branches; or bypass SymPy entirely for MIPS scoring and
        evaluate expression strings with the existing safe NumPy operator
        namespace.
  \item Use the same faithful evaluator for Pareto validation, full-relation
        matching, and selected-equation accuracy.  Display-oriented symbolic
        conversion should not be a prerequisite for behavioral scoring.
  \item Add cross-backend tests comparing Julia and Python/SymPy behavior on
        zero and near-zero denominators, non-integer intermediates, \texttt{nan},
        infinities, and nested operators.
  \item Record the failing stage, complete exception, and offending equation;
        retain the hall of fame on failures.  Do not silently turn match-check
        exceptions into ordinary non-matches.
  \item Rerun all 270 baseline fits after the repair.  Rerunning only the 48
        explicit errors from the 126-result diagnostic snapshot would miss
        possible false non-matches that were swallowed inside the checker.
\end{enumerate}

The diagnostic snapshot before cancellation contained 126 result files: 58
strict exact hits, 20 valid-looking non-solves, and 48 explicit post-search
errors.  Those counts are useful for diagnosing the adapter, but they are not a
clean baseline comparison.
